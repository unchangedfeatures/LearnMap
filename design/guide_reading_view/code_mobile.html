Understanding the Bell Curve

The normal distribution, often called the bell curve, is one of the most important concepts in statistics. It describes how traits or variables are distributed within a population. In a perfectly normal distribution, the mean, median, and mode are all equal and located at the center of the distribution.

Most of the data points fall close to the mean. As you move further away from the mean in either direction, the frequency of data points decreases, creating the characteristic bell shape.

The empirical rule, or the 68-95-99.7 rule, tells us that for a normal distribution, roughly 68% of the data falls within one standard deviation of the mean, 95% within two standard deviations, and 99.7% within three standard deviations.

Real-world Example

Think about adult human height. Most people are clustered around average height. There are fewer very tall or very short people, and extremely tall or extremely short people are rare. If you plotted the heights of a large random sample of adults, the resulting graph would closely resemble a bell curve.

Key Takeaway

The standard deviation controls the spread. A small standard deviation makes the bell tall and narrow; a large one makes it short and wide. Regardless of spread, the area under the curve always equals 1 (or 100%).
